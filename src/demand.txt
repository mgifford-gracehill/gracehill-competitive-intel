GRACE HILL — DEMAND STRATEGY 2H 2026
The Play Book
Organised by segment and persona. Every play sells a bundle, not a product.
We are moving from selling individual products to selling outcome bundles. That changes who we sell to and what we say, so this playbook is organised the way the selling will work: by segment, then by the persona who owns the budget, then by the bundle motion.
This describes a future state. The bundles are not launched, final contents are still being agreed, and it is not yet proven that this industry is ready to buy outcomes rather than products. Part Eight covers that risk directly and proposes plays to test it. Everything here should be read as the model we are aligning on, not the model we are running.
This document exists so that sales and marketing agree one go-to-market model — the same segments, the same personas, the same qualification bar and the same rules about who contacts whom.
How to find your play
The three bundles every play sells
Add-ons — additional shops, extra modules, certifications and Realync — attach to an existing bundle rather than being sold on their own. They are covered in Part Five-B.
Two rules that apply to every play
No account enters a play without a recorded, dated reason to reach out. Not a segment. Not a persona match. A specific event or gap a person would recognise as a reason to make contact.
Never pitch the second bundle to the buyer who owns the first. Each bundle has its own budget owner. Selling Resident & Prospect to an L&D leader asks them to spend a budget they do not control.

Part Five — Plays That Run Across Every Segment
X1 — Re-Engagement
Any segment · Buyer: whoever we last spoke to · 139 opportunities, $1.6M
Half of all lost revenue went to no decision rather than to a competitor. A competitive loss needs a better product or price; a no-decision loss needs presence until conditions change. This is also the fastest play to start — the accounts are already qualified and it depends on none of the data fixes.
X2 — Bundle Migration
Every existing customer · Buyer: the current bundle owner, then the counterpart
Three risks: customers feeling they now pay for capability they do not use, which the discovery window answers only if usage is surfaced; a window that expires silently, which reads as a takeaway; and migration landing as a price conversation instead of an outcome conversation. The third is marketing's to own.
Part Five-B — Add-Ons
Add-ons attach to a bundle an account already holds. None of them is sold on its own, and the buyer is always whoever owns the bundle it attaches to.
Realync, specifically
Realync is the only product in the portfolio going backwards — revenue fell 7.7% last year. External research explains why: buyers already get virtual leasing through platforms they own, so it loses a category evaluation on its own merits. The fix is distribution, not the product.
Never sold standalone. It attaches to Resident & Prospect Satisfaction, where the marketing buyer is already ours.
The evidence comes from a product they own — a conversion gap their own prospect shopping or survey results have already surfaced.
It is the only product with quantified ROI proof points already built, which is exactly what an attach motion needs and which is currently wasted on cold prospects who have no reason to believe us yet.
An add-on to an existing agreement is a much smaller decision than a platform evaluation, which removes the evaluation Realync loses.
Realync runs a separate sales team and system. This play needs an agreed handoff — who opens the conversation, who prices it, and whose number it lands on — settled before launch, not after.
Part Six — Win-Backs and Closed-Lost
This is the most under-invested motion we have. Win-back opportunities convert at 76.5% at an average won value of $29,127 — higher than our average new-logo deal — and we run twenty-six of them in total. The re-approachable lost pool is 4,252 opportunities worth $60.9M.
Two caveats before this is quoted anywhere. The 76.5% comes from a very small base, so treat it as encouraging rather than proven. And lapsed customers average 2,358 units against 7,504 for active ones — the churned base is large in count but small in size, so it is a volume play, not an enterprise one.
Why we lose, all time — and what each loss needs
Read the first row before designing any discount. No-decision losses are 19.3% of lost value; price and budget together are 5.8%. Discounting is the wrong instrument for roughly nine out of ten lost deals, and using it there gives away margin to solve a problem we do not have.

This play requires something we do not have: a maintained register of what we lost on, mapped against what we have since shipped. Building it is a small piece of work with a direct line to $7.8M of already-qualified demand, and it should sit with product marketing.
W2 — The Consolidation That Underdelivered

The bundles make this play possible in a way it was not before. We lost these accounts as a collection of point products; we can now re-approach as a platform. Consolidation is also our single largest churn cause, so this play defends as well as it wins.
W3 — Competitive Displacement
Win-back · 756 named-competitor losses · $19.1M · 3x richer per deal than average
Our competitive data is weak. Only 5.7% of lost opportunities name a real competitor and 91.4% are recorded as unknown, none or other. Before scaling this play, competitor capture at close needs to become mandatory — otherwise we are targeting the small share we happened to write down.
W4 — Budget Re-Entry
Win-back · 472 opportunities · $6.0M · Where discounting belongs, and only here
Discounting rules for win-backs
Change the offer before changing the price. A smaller bundle they will use beats a discounted bundle they will not.
Never discount a no-decision loss. They did not decline on price, and a discount teaches them that waiting produces one.
If a concession is necessary, trade it — a multi-year term, a reference or case-study commitment, an earlier start date, or expanded scope. A concession given for nothing re-anchors every future renewal.
Time-box any re-entry offer and tie it to the bundle migration window, so it reads as a moment rather than a standing position.
Set approval bands and expiry dates before the programme launches, not deal by deal.
Track discount depth against retention. A discount that buys a customer who churns in eighteen months is a loss booked as a win.
The pricing research supports this framing. Money accounts for roughly 11% of churned revenue and is defined there as delinquent billing and absent budget rather than price resistance — so price is rarely the real objection even when it is the recorded one.
W5 — Champion Movement
Win-back · Untracked today · The highest-converting trigger in B2B
This is the cheapest play in the book and the only one currently running at zero. It needs contact-level job-change monitoring, which is a standard data service rather than a build.
W6 — Lapsed Customer Reactivation
Win-back · 2,425 accounts · Volume play, not an enterprise one
Data limitation to fix first: only 2.9% of lapsed accounts record what they used to own, and no reliable churn date exists. Without those two fields this play cannot be segmented, and it will read as generic. Backfilling from historic closed-won records is the prerequisite.
Part Seven — Channel Strategy: Who Reaches Out, and How
The most common failure in a motion like this is not a bad campaign. It is marketing and sales contacting the same person in the same week with different messages. This part sets who owns which channel and the rules where they overlap.
The three layers
Rules where the layers overlap
Marketing suppresses any account in an active sales sequence from competing campaigns. One voice at a time.
Sales does not sequence an account without a marketing-supplied reason to reach out on the record.
When sales sequences an account, marketing runs advertising to the same roles for the duration. The call is never the first contact.
Customer accounts route to the account owner, never to a new-logo sequence. This is the failure the whole strategy exists to fix.
Both sides record outcomes in the same place. A reply to an SDR and a reply to a campaign are the same signal about the same account.
Marketing outreach versus sales outreach
Deploying conversation intelligence, AI SDR and live SDR
We have three outbound capabilities and they are good at different things. Using the wrong one is expensive in both directions — AI on an enterprise account reads as spam, and a live rep on a 200-unit reactivation does not pay for itself.
The most under-used asset we have is what is said on calls. We already target advertising at accounts where a specific product came up in a recorded conversation — that practice works and it currently applies to one product. Extended across the portfolio it becomes a trigger source for the entire trigger library, and it is the only signal that captures intent nobody clicked on.
Channels and strategies we are not currently considering
Named plainly, because several are larger than things we are actively investing in.
If only three are picked up: lapsed customers, job-change monitoring and conversation intelligence. All three are low effort, all three use assets we already hold, and none requires a new channel to be built.
Part Eight — Is the Market Ready to Buy This Way?
Everything in this book describes a future state. The bundles are not launched, and it is genuinely unclear whether this industry is ready to buy outcomes rather than products. That uncertainty deserves plays of its own rather than an assumption.
What we know, and what we are assuming
Plays to build readiness

R2 — Lead With a Benchmark Only We Can Produce
Market readiness · The proof is the product
R3 — Let the Base Make the Argument
Market readiness · 328 customers already buy this way
R4 — Let Them Experience It Rather Than Argue About It
Market readiness · The discovery window
The window only works if usage is surfaced. An unused window converts nobody and an unmeasured one cannot be argued from — and a window that expires silently reads as a takeaway rather than a trial.
How we will know whether the market is ready
Do buyers use outcome language back to us on calls, or do they keep asking for products by name?
Does the counterpart persona engage with a benchmark aimed at their function, or only the buyer we already have?
Does the discovery window get used, or ignored?
Do multi-bundle deals close at a comparable rate to single-bundle deals, or materially slower?
Recommend agreeing in advance what would cause us to slow down. If multi-bundle deals take materially longer to close without a higher win rate, the right response is to keep bundles as a pricing and packaging construct while continuing to sell them one at a time — a legitimate outcome, and much cheaper to discover early than late.
Part Nine — How Accounts Reach a Play
Every play above assumes an account arrives qualified. There are two ways that happens, and they share one target list, one gate and one scorecard.
The qualification gate
An account qualifies on breadth first and score second. Our contact-level score rates almost every contact identically, so it cannot discriminate and cannot be the gate. Two roles engaged at one account is a signal no single contact score can fake.
Score does not open the gate. It ranks priority inside the qualified pool, so the team works the warmest accounts first. Thresholds are a starting proposal to calibrate against actual conversion in the first quarter.
Inbound — earning the second role
The job is not to score one person higher. It is to get a colleague of theirs to engage.
Customers register for our webinars as training. Count a customer webinar registration as a role engaged. Never count it as intent to buy.

Appendix — Data Notes
Three known distortions. Two of them make the opportunity look larger than it is, which is why they are stated plainly.
Market data and Realync are under-counted
Both run on separate systems with separate sales teams. Market data has no records in the main system at all; Realync appears at roughly half its true customer count. True bundle attachment is therefore somewhat higher, and whitespace somewhat smaller, than shown. Single-bundle share is realistically 66–70% rather than exactly 69.6%.
Mystery shopping growth is overstated
It shows 68% year-on-year growth, a large share of which is existing on-demand customers converted to annual contracts with a minimum spend. The revenue is real; the growth rate is not repeatable, and the cross-sell case rests on attach evidence rather than on that number.
Pipeline sources disagree
Our two pipeline views differ materially, most sharply on mid-market cross-sell, almost certainly a difference in how renewal records are treated. Volume and totals in this book come from the core system; mix and win rates from the revenue view. Neither overrides the other and they are never combined in one table.
Other caveats
Win rates use closed opportunities only. 71% of opportunities created this year are still open, so all rates will move.
Re-engagement figures use a cleaned pool of 324 lost opportunities worth $3.27M, after excluding 212 duplicate records that inflated the raw pool by 41%.
Sales cycle is not reported anywhere in this book because it cannot currently be computed.
Willingness-to-pay research was designed but has not yet been delivered. No claim here depends on it.
