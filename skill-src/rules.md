# The rules, and why each one exists

Every one of these came from something going wrong. They are not style preferences.

## Never present Unknown as a No

`Unknown` means no public source states it either way. `No` means a source says they do not
do it. Turning the first into the second is the single most common way this data gets a
person into trouble, because it is a claim that cannot be defended when challenged.

Correct: *"Nothing they publish says whether they do X — worth asking them."*
Wrong: *"They can't do X."*

## Never name a Grace Hill customer or prospect

In prose or in a URL slug. The only exception is an approved, attributed public case study.
If a source document names an account, strip it before using anything from that document.

## Do not widen what a source says

This is the failure that has caused the most trouble. A source supports exactly what it says
and nothing broader.

- "Approved in the State of Texas" does **not** support "state-approved".
- "Mystery shop results exist" does **not** support "we measure competency".
- "We monitor regulatory change" does **not** support "we keep you jurisdiction-current".

If you catch yourself widening a noun — one state to states, one signal to a category, a
process to a guarantee — stop and write the narrow version.

## Never name an authority the source does not name

If a page says "approved in the State of Texas", write that. Do not supply the agency, the
acronym or the statute because one plausibly exists. A specific-sounding regulator is the
highest-risk thing this material can invent, because it reads as *more* rigorous than the
truth it replaced.

## Three kinds of claim, and they are not interchangeable

- **Sourced** — a named source with a URL says it. Safe to state.
- **Our read** — our conclusion from the record. Useful for shaping an argument, not for
  presenting as a published fact.
- **Our positioning** — a competitive judgment we made: a ranking, a characterization.
  Legitimate, and it must never wear the clothes of a sourced fact.

When you write anything from this data, keep the distinction visible in the output.

## Roadmap never reaches the field

Only shipped, available capability may be described as capability. No dates, no quarters, no
"coming soon". Anything from a discovery board is somebody thinking and is not roadmap at all.

## Grace Hill maintains the content; the customer keeps the obligation

Never write anything implying Grace Hill assumes, transfers or guarantees a customer's
compliance responsibility. Our legal monitoring is ongoing review with action when
appropriate — not a guarantee that every regulatory development produces an immediate
content change.

## Partners compete, and that is normal

A company can be a partner on one product and a competitor on another. Compete on the
overlapping product, never on the parent company. Never disparage a partner and never
discuss commercial terms of a partnership.

## Companies that are never competitors

Edge2Learn, Ellis Partners, EPMS, HelloData, Realync, SkillCat, Visto, Kingsley. These are
Grace Hill companies. Never write competitive content about them.

## Never publish a competitor's private numbers

Their price, customer count, headcount or course count may only be used if they publish it
themselves. Cite where, and carry the date.

## Dates matter as much as sources

A 2017 approval under a predecessor product name is a different fact from a current one.
Carry dates. If a fact has no date, say that it has no date — that is itself the finding.

## Source hierarchy, when they disagree

1. Grace Hill University (internal policy) · 2. AdminHQ (product documentation) ·
3. catalog.gracehill.com · 4. Release notes · 5. RFP responses — context only, never
authoritative · 6. gracehill.com marketing pages.

Higher rank wins. Never average two conflicting sources into a confident middle.
