# Battlecard design — what the field says works

Research summary, 2026-08-11. Sources at the bottom.

## The one finding that matters

Every credible source converges on the same constraint: **a battlecard is read under time pressure, or it is not read at all.** Klue's rule is blunt — "One page is the absolute standard," and "if sellers cannot scan a battlecard and find their answer in under 60 seconds, the card is too long." Klue's own history of the format is a warning: it started as a single-sided piece of paper, grew to both sides, then became "100-slide decks, that sit on network drives and are viewed by no one."

Forrester's critique is structural rather than cosmetic. Their argument is that battlecards stayed frozen in an era when "customers purchased one product over another based on a side-by-side comparison of their features," and that the term itself has been diluted to cover industry overviews, tech profiles and partnership decks until reps no longer know what they're opening. Their recommendation is to stop equipping reps to push products and start helping them "sell outcomes, solutions, or business benefits."

Put together: **a feature matrix is not a battlecard.** A feature matrix can live *inside* one, below the fold, as evidence.

## Recommended section order

Ordered by what a rep needs first when the competitor's name lands on a live call.

| # | Section | Why it goes here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Verdict / stance — one sentence** | The single highest-value line on the card. Klue's "Fact" level: the competitive insight in one sentence, before any explanation. If the rep reads nothing else, they read this. |
| 2 | **Am I actually facing this?** | Klue's content framework opens with "Identify the Competitor" for a reason — reps frequently misidentify who they're up against. Include the aka names, the parent, the tell-tale signals. |
| 3 | **The quick dismiss / so-what** | Klue's "Impact" level: why the fact matters *to us, in the field.* A two-line rebuttal that redirects to customer needs. This is the part reps actually memorise. |
| 4 | **Questions to ask** | Klue's "Act" level and PMA's list both put discovery before comparison. Trap-setting questions phrased for a live call — Klue calls these "landmines to lay." Questions travel better than claims because they don't expose the rep to a factual counter. |
| 5 | **Why we win** | Approved, outcome-framed claims. Forrester's point applies: business benefit, not feature. |
| 6 | **Objection handling** | Klue's "Defend." Buyer language on the left, rep response on the right. Reps hit this section reactively, so it must be findable without reading sections 1–5. |
| 7 | **When NOT to fight / disqualify** | See below — the most under-served section in the standard templates and the most useful one. |
| 8 | **Their strengths, honestly stated** | Deliberately below the fold. Klue's research (Brock Everett, Shopify) found reps skip competitor background — but an honest strength list is what stops a rep getting blindsided, and it earns trust with a prospect who already uses the product. Keep it short and sourced. |
| 9 | **Proof / evidence** | Klue's "Show": verifiable third-party material. Klue is explicit that cards should show sources and separate unsubstantiated claims from validated intel, because "out-of-date content destroys the trust between the prospect and your sales rep." |
| 10 | **Feature comparison** | Last, always. Klue warns that "if your reps are selling on features — they will always end up in a battle to the lowest price." Reference material, not the pitch. |
| 11 | **Do not say** | A short liability list. Not in the vendor templates; it belongs on any card where a rep could state an unsourced absolute negative. |
| 12 | **Freshness stamp + owner** | Last reviewed, by whom. Klue treats currency as a trust precondition, not metadata. |

Sections 1–4 must fit above the fold. Playwise's guidance is that "the most critical battlecard real estate is what's visible without scrolling."

### The underlying frameworks

Klue offers two, and they nest into the order above:

- **Fact → Impact → Act.** Fact = the insight in one sentence. Impact = the "so what," i.e. why it matters to *our* teams. Act = the talk track, itself in three parts — **Prompt** (opening question), **Follow up** (the positioning narrative), **Validate** (closing question confirming relevance).
- **Know → Say → Show.** Know = background and leading questions. Say = the concise, impact-focused play. Show = verifiable evidence establishing credibility.

Klue's three content tests for any line on a card: **Context** (include the "so what?"), **Charge** (language carrying positive or negative charge to guide positioning), **Specificity** (concrete metrics, not vague claims).

## Length and format

- **One page.** Klue states it as the standard. Dock's survey of 24 real cards found a 1–6 page range in practice but still recommends a "short-form pitch your sales reps can deliver in under a minute."
- **Two thresholds to design against** (Playwise): readable in 60 seconds; a *specific* answer retrievable in 10.
- **Digital-first, not print-first.** Readable without zooming, no horizontal scroll. Tabbed navigation (Overview / Objections / Win-loss / Comparison) beats one long scroll for "fast recall under pressure."
- **Skip the design flourishes.** Dock: "Use headers, short bullet points, and clear sections. Skip heavy design, clever layouts, or dense tables."
- **Structure consistently across every card.** Same sections in the same order every time — reps navigate by muscle memory, and Crayon adds that consistent headers and clean modular sections (simple Markdown or HTML) also make cards machine-readable for AI retrieval.

## Visual hierarchy and color

- **Typography carries the hierarchy:** multiple header levels, clear font-size steps, and bolding of *key terms only* — never whole sentences, which destroys scannability.
- **Color coding, three roles maximum** (Playwise): green for advantages, red for threats, blue for neutral fact. Cap the palette at three or four colors; more becomes visual noise. Never let color be the only carrier of meaning — pair it with a label or icon for accessibility.
- **One page beats two.** If the content genuinely needs two, that is the signal to split into two *cards* (e.g. a competitor card and a pricing card) rather than one longer document. Klue lists six card types precisely so no single card has to carry everything: Competitor, Objection Handling, What Prospects Are Saying, Product, Why We Win, and Pricing & Packaging.

## What to leave out

- **Competitor company background.** Klue's most-cited field quote, from Brock Everett at Shopify: "there seemed to be a lot of focus on the background of the competitor, their leadership, where they were founded, and what their financials were like. Sales reps are just skipping through all this." Founding year, HQ and funding belong in the record, not on the card face.
- **Exhaustive feature matrices.** Klue warns against cards that pack in "every feature comparison, every data point, and every possible objection" — this is the single most common failure mode.
- **Undated or unsourced claims.** Stale intel is worse than no intel.
- **Bare bullet lists with no narrative.** Klue explicitly cautions against "a list of bullet points" alone; reps remember content through the FIA structure.
- **Executive-register prose.** Write the words the rep will actually say out loud. Klue: "You need to tell them exactly what to say."
- **Vague language.** Crayon: "Avoid vague language — use specific, sharp phrasing."

## Handling a competitor you do NOT compete with

There is **no established template** for this. PMA's eleven-section list, Klue's FIA framework and Crayon's blueprint all assume head-to-head framing, and Klue's own guides state no guidance for non-direct competitors. But the adjacent, well-supported pattern is **disqualification**, and it transfers cleanly.

Dock's survey found this explicitly: an effective battlecard "spells out exactly when to compete and when to gracefully bow out," with named signals — deal-size limits, missing compliance requirements, "technical requirements you can't meet without custom work," and "buyer profiles that churn." Infoblox's card teaches reps to recognize which "problem states" are worth pursuing; Pipe17's makes it "obvious when a deal is broken."

Applied to a non-competitor, the working pattern is:

1. **Lead with the stance, not the comparison.** State plainly that this is not a fight, and why — different buyer, different price point, different job to be done. This *is* the "Fact" line.
2. **Name the actual boundary.** The concrete, sourced dividing line: who they sell to, at what price, for what decision. Specificity is what makes the rep believe it.
3. **Replace "why we win" with "what to do instead."** The rep still needs an action. Not a rebuttal — a redirect: acknowledge the tool is fine for its job, then reframe toward the decision your product serves.
4. **Give permission to concede.** Explicitly authorise the rep to say "that's a good tool for that" — this is the highest-credibility move available and reps won't make it without cover.
5. **Flag the drift condition.** State what would change the verdict (moving upmarket, an enterprise tier, an acquisition) so the card has a trigger for re-review rather than silently going stale.
6. **Keep the feature matrix, shrink it.** It answers "are we sure?" for the rep and for whoever reviews the card — but it should not be the frame.

The cost of forcing a non-competitor into head-to-head framing is not just wasted space: it teaches the rep to attack a product the prospect may reasonably like, which reads as uninformed and burns credibility. Forrester's outcome-over-product argument is the deeper justification — if you're not competing for the same outcome, a feature comparison was never the right instrument.
